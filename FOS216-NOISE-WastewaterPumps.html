Regulatory Basis — What "Threshold" Means in Cleveland

CMC §605.10 / Zoning Performance Standards / OEPA PTIO

Cleveland's Codified Ordinances do not set a single numeric fence-line decibel limit the way some suburban zoning codes do. The applicable framework is a layered one, and the mitigation design below is built to clear the strictest layer with margin — consistent with the design basis used across the FOS216-NOISE series.

Layer 1 — CMC §605.10, Unnecessary Noise

Cleveland's general noise ordinance prohibits "unreasonably loud, disturbing and unnecessary noise… detrimental to the life and health of any individual, or [that] unreasonably disturb[s] the quiet, comfort, or repose of neighboring inhabitants." This is a qualitative/nuisance standard, not a posted dB(A) number — enforcement is complaint- and officer-judgment driven.

Layer 2 — Zoning Performance Standards

Industrial-district performance standards require noise be "muffled so as not to become objectionable due to intermittence, beat frequency, shrillness, or intensity," measured at the property line of the tract. No fixed number is published; the standard is character-based — and "intermittence" is directly relevant here, since lift-station and transfer pumps cycle on/off rather than running continuously.

Design Basis Adopted for This Project

Because Cleveland's own code is qualitative, FOS216 designs hyperscale wastewater pump installations to the numeric target most commonly cited by Ohio EPA and used as the de facto industrial fence-line standard in this region — the same target applied across the FOS216-NOISE series:

55 dB(A) Leq — daytime (7:00 a.m.–10:00 p.m.)  ·  50 dB(A) Leq — nighttime (10:00 p.m.–7:00 a.m.), measured at the nearest sensitive receptor or property line, whichever is more restrictive.

This target is conservative relative to Cleveland's actual enforcement standard and is the number used throughout this document. It is a design target, not a legal limit recited from Cleveland's code — treat it as the engineering basis that keeps the project well clear of a §605.10 nuisance complaint, not as a quoted ordinance figure.

Two additional touch points apply to a hyperscale wastewater pump installation specifically:

  • Cycling/intermittent operation is the controlling complaint risk, not steady-state Leq: lift station and transfer pumps start and stop with wet-well level or treatment-train batch timing, often including off-hours. A pump bank that's quiet on average but produces a sharp on/off transient at 2:00 a.m. is exactly the "intermittence" pattern Cleveland's zoning language calls out, and is statistically the most common driver of industrial noise complaints regardless of the underlying Leq number.
  • Ohio EPA NPDES / PTI coordination: the wastewater pump station itself is not separately air- or noise-permitted, but where it serves a permitted discharge system (consistent with the FOS216 PFAS treatment train design basis already on file for this project), OEPA and Cleveland Division of Water Pollution Control review of the pump station's site plan commonly requests the same fence-line noise impact confirmation used for the mechanical yard.

Noise Source Profile — Industrial Wastewater Pumps

Vertical turbine lift station + horizontal centrifugal transfer pumps

A hyperscale wastewater pump room mixes several distinct noise mechanisms across the lift station and treatment-train transfer pumps. Unlike the cooling tower (one dominant splash mechanism) or the transformer (one dominant tone), pump noise is a composite of motor, hydraulic, and cavitation sources that each need separate attention.

Source PointMechanismTypical Unmitigated LevelDominant Frequency
Motor noise (electromagnetic + cooling fan) Stator slot frequency tones + shaft-mounted cooling fan, scales with HP 72–82 dB(A) @ 3 ft, large units (≥200 HP) Broadband + tonal at pole-pass frequency
Hydraulic / flow noise Turbulent flow through volute, impeller vane-pass interaction 70–78 dB(A) @ 3 ft Vane-pass tone (impeller blades × RPM) + broadband
Cavitation (off-design operation) Vapor bubble collapse when pump runs outside best-efficiency point, common at low wet-well level 80–90+ dB(A) @ 3 ft when present Broadband, harsh/crackling character, high-frequency emphasis
Discharge piping / check valve slam Water hammer at pump start/stop, check valve closure on flow reversal Impulsive, up to 95 dB(A) peak (not Leq) Broadband impulse, very short duration
Building/structure-borne transmission Vibration through pump base, piping penetrations, and pump room walls into the building envelope Path-dependent; can dominate exterior level if untreated Low-frequency emphasis (structure-borne)
Why Cavitation and Water Hammer Are the Real Design Drivers

Steady-state, correctly-sized pump noise at the fence line is rarely the problem — a pump room is typically indoors, and geometric spreading plus one building wall already does most of the work. The actual risk is off-design operation: cavitation from a wet-well level control problem, or check-valve slam from a poorly sequenced VFD ramp-down, both of which produce sudden, loud, impulsive noise events that are exactly the "intermittence" pattern the zoning code penalizes — and exactly the kind of event a neighbor calls in on. Correct hydraulic design and control sequencing prevent more fence-line noise risk here than any acoustic hardware does.

The Steady-State Number Is Not the Compliance Risk

At 25.7 dB(A), this installation clears the 55/50 dB(A) target with enormous margin under normal operation — the indoor enclosure alone does most of the work. The compliance risk this document actually needs to manage is the off-design transient case: a cavitating pump running 10–15 dB louder than the design-duty source level above, combined with a check-valve slam event that can add a further impulsive 10–20 dB peak (not Leq, but audible and complaint-generating regardless). A pump room engineered only to the steady-state number and left without proper level control, VFD ramp logic, and valve selection can still generate a fence-line nuisance complaint even though its "on-paper" noise budget looks comfortable. Mitigation below is sequenced to close that gap, not to chase the already-compliant steady-state figure.

04

Mitigation Measures — Ranked by Cost-Effectiveness

Apply in order; stop once target + margin is met

Measures below are sequenced to close the actual risk gap identified in Section 03 first — controlling cavitation and water hammer — before standard acoustic hardware, which is the reverse priority from a source that's continuously noisy at steady state.

Wet-Well Level Control / Anti-Cavitation Sequencing01
−10–15 dB(A) eliminates cavitation event entirely vs. partial mitigation

Properly tuned level transmitters and lead/lag pump staging logic that keeps each pump within its best-efficiency-point flow range and never allows run-dry or low-NPSH operation. This is a controls and hydraulic-design fix, not acoustic hardware — it removes the loudest possible event from the noise budget entirely rather than attenuating it after the fact. Zero incremental hardware cost if designed in from the start; expensive to retrofit if undersized piping or wet-well geometry forces marginal NPSH at any operating point.

SOURCE TREATMENTCONTROLS / HYDRAULIC DESIGN
Slow-Closing / Non-Slam Check Valves02
−15–20 dB peak eliminates water hammer impulse event

Spring-loaded silent/non-slam check valves or VFD-controlled soft-stop ramping in place of conventional swing checks. Water hammer is an impulsive event, not a steady Leq contributor, but it is exactly the kind of "intermittent" sharp noise that drives nuisance complaints — specify at the same procurement stage as the pumps themselves, since retrofit means a piping shutdown.

SOURCE TREATMENTPROCUREMENT-STAGE
Pump Room Envelope & Penetration Sealing03
−20–25 dB(A) interior-to-exterior transmission, as modeled in Section 03

Solid-core or acoustic-rated door (STC 35+), sealed conduit/piping penetrations with acoustic sealant, and a CMU or equivalent-mass exterior wall. This is the single highest-leverage measure for steady-state pump noise and is typically achieved by standard mechanical room construction practice — the design discipline is making sure penetrations don't become flanking paths that undo the wall's performance.

ENVELOPESTANDARD CONSTRUCTION PRACTICE
Pump/Motor Base Vibration Isolation04
−5–8 dB(A) structure-borne path into building/piping

Spring or elastomeric isolators between the pump baseplate and housekeeping pad, plus flexible pipe connectors at suction and discharge to prevent vibration transmission through rigid piping runs into the building structure and ultimately the exterior envelope. Without this, structure-borne transmission can flank the envelope treatment in Measure 03 entirely.

PATH TREATMENTRETROFIT-CAPABLE
VFD Speed Control / Soft-Start05
−4–7 dB(A) at part-load operation, vs. across-the-line start/stop

Variable-frequency drives running pumps at the minimum speed that meets actual flow demand rather than full-speed on/off cycling. Reduces both motor noise and the abruptness of the start/stop transient that drives the intermittence-related complaint risk, while also reducing the wear that leads to cavitation and check-valve slam over the equipment's service life.

OPERATIONALRELIABILITY SYNERGY
Discharge Pipe Lagging / Acoustic Wrap06
−5–10 dB(A) localized, where piping exits the building envelope

Reserved for sites where piping must exit the pump room near the property line or where an exposed exterior discharge run carries audible flow noise — mass-loaded vinyl or mineral-wool lagging wrapped around the pipe run, particularly at any point where it penetrates the exterior wall and becomes a flanking noise path around the envelope treatment.

This monitoring array shares architecture with the cooling tower (FOS216-NOISE-004) and HV transformer (FOS216-NOISE-005) modules — fence-line stations report into the same Cityworks-style asset/event model, with peak/impulse logging added here specifically to catch the intermittent water-hammer and cavitation events that are the controlling risk for this source.
