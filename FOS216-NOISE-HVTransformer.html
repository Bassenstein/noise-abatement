01

Regulatory Basis — What "Threshold" Means in Cleveland

CMC §605.10 / Zoning Performance Standards / OEPA PTIO

Cleveland's Codified Ordinances do not set a single numeric fence-line decibel limit the way some suburban zoning codes do. The applicable framework is a layered one, and the mitigation design below is built to clear the strictest layer with margin — consistent with the design basis used across the FOS216-NOISE series.

Layer 1 — CMC §605.10, Unnecessary Noise

Cleveland's general noise ordinance prohibits "unreasonably loud, disturbing and unnecessary noise… detrimental to the life and health of any individual, or [that] unreasonably disturb[s] the quiet, comfort, or repose of neighboring inhabitants." This is a qualitative/nuisance standard, not a posted dB(A) number — enforcement is complaint- and officer-judgment driven.

Layer 2 — Zoning Performance Standards

Industrial-district performance standards require noise be "muffled so as not to become objectionable due to intermittence, beat frequency, shrillness, or intensity," measured at the property line of the tract. No fixed number is published; the standard is character-based — and notably, "beat frequency" and "shrillness" map directly onto transformer hum, which is discussed below.

Design Basis Adopted for This Project

Because Cleveland's own code is qualitative, FOS216 designs hyperscale electrical yards to the numeric target most commonly cited by Ohio EPA and used as the de facto industrial fence-line standard in this region — the same target applied across the FOS216-NOISE series:

55 dB(A) Leq — daytime (7:00 a.m.–10:00 p.m.)  ·  50 dB(A) Leq — nighttime (10:00 p.m.–7:00 a.m.), measured at the nearest sensitive receptor or property line, whichever is more restrictive.

This target is conservative relative to Cleveland's actual enforcement standard and is the number used throughout this document. It is a design target, not a legal limit recited from Cleveland's code — treat it as the engineering basis that keeps the project well clear of a §605.10 nuisance complaint, not as a quoted ordinance figure.

Two additional touch points apply to a hyperscale electrical yard specifically:

  • Tonal penalty exposure is higher here than for most sources: transformer hum is a pure 120 Hz tone (in the U.S. 60 Hz grid, the magnetostriction-driven hum doubles the line frequency) plus harmonics at 240/360/480 Hz. Tonal noise is penalized in most noise impact methodologies (commonly a +5 dB adjustment) precisely because the qualitative language in §605.10 — "intermittence, beat frequency, shrillness" — was written with tones like this in mind. Treat the design target as 50/45 dB(A) effective once the tonal adjustment is backed out, not 55/50.
  • Ohio EPA PTIO / utility interconnection review: air permitting does not touch transformer noise, but large step-up transformer installations (typically ≥50 MVA at hyperscale scale) are frequently flagged in utility interconnection and CPC site plan review for fence-line noise impact, particularly where oil-filled units sit near a residential parcel.
02

Noise Source Profile — HV Transformer

Oil-filled, ONAN/ONAF, pad-mount and substation-class units

Transformer noise comes from two mechanically distinct sources that behave very differently and require separate mitigation: core hum (continuous, tonal, present at all load) and cooling-fan noise (load-dependent, broadband, intermittent). A correct mitigation plan treats them as two separate problems, not one.

Source PointMechanismTypical Unmitigated LevelDominant Frequency
Core magnetostriction hum Core laminations expand/contract at 2× line frequency under magnetic flux 65–75 dB(A) @ 3 ft, scales with MVA 120 Hz fundamental + 240/360/480 Hz harmonics
Winding vibration Load-current-driven Lorentz-force vibration in windings, transmitted through tank wall +3–6 dB(A) above core hum at full load 120 Hz, load-modulated
Cooling fan banks (ONAF/OFAF) Forced-air radiator fans, staged on with load and ambient temperature 68–78 dB(A) @ 3 ft per fan bank Broadband, 200 Hz–2 kHz
Tank wall radiation Core/winding vibration re-radiated as airborne noise from tank surfaces and radiator fins Dominant path for total dB(A) at distance 120 Hz tonal + broadband fan content
Why Transformer Noise Is Different From Mechanical Noise

Cooling tower or generator noise is broadband and largely "fixable" with mufflers and barriers, which attenuate fairly evenly across frequency. Transformer hum is a narrow tone at 120 Hz — a frequency band that standard acoustic barriers and enclosures are comparatively poor at attenuating (low-frequency wavelengths are long, and mass-law barrier performance drops at low frequency). This is the single most important design fact in this document: the fix has to attack the source (core design, mounting) more than the path, which is the opposite emphasis from the cooling tower and generator modules in this series.

Why Geometry Alone Usually Isn't the Whole Answer

Unlike the cooling tower case, the worked example above already clears the daytime target with no acoustic hardware — geometric spreading plus the tonal penalty still lands under 55 dB(A). The risk profile for transformers is different: it's not the Leq number that drives complaints, it's the tonal character itself. A 120 Hz hum that's 8–10 dB below the broadband ambient is still often audible and identifiable as "the hum" to a nearby resident, because the ear is highly sensitive to tonal content against a broadband background — this is exactly the "shrillness" and "beat frequency" language in Cleveland's zoning performance standard. Treat the dB(A) number as necessary but not sufficient; the mitigation measures below exist primarily to break the tonal signature, not just lower the level.

04

Mitigation Measures — Ranked by Cost-Effectiveness

Apply in order; stop once target + margin is met

Measures below are sequenced cheapest/most-effective first, with explicit separation between core-hum (tonal, source-side) and fan-noise (broadband, path-side) treatments — the two require different approaches.

Low-Noise Core Design (Procurement Spec)01
−8–12 dB(A) at source, core hum component

Specify reduced core flux density, step-lap (mitered) core joints, and laser-scribed grain-oriented steel at the time of transformer order — this is the highest-leverage fix available and the only one that addresses the 120 Hz tone at its origin rather than downstream. Cannot be retrofitted; must be a line item in the transformer technical specification (NEMA TR-1 sound level class specified explicitly, not left to vendor default).

SOURCE TREATMENTPROCUREMENT-STAGE ONLY
Anti-Vibration Mounts / Tank Isolation02
−4–7 dB(A) structure-borne path, winding vibration

Elastomeric or spring isolators between the transformer base and concrete pad break the structure-borne transmission path from winding vibration into the foundation and surrounding slab — without isolation, vibration re-radiates as noise well beyond the tank itself via the pad and any connected conduit/bus duct. Retrofit-capable but disruptive (requires temporary unit removal or jacking).

PATH TREATMENTRETROFIT: DISRUPTIVE
Acoustic Barrier Wall, Substation Perimeter03
−3–6 dB(A) fan-band frequencies; minimal effect on 120 Hz tone

A solid barrier at the electrical yard perimeter, sized so the top breaks line-of-sight from the transformer bank to the nearest receptor. Effective against the broadband fan-noise component; largely ineffective against the 120 Hz core hum due to the long wavelength (~9.4 ft at 120 Hz) relative to typical barrier height — do not rely on this measure alone to solve a tonal complaint. Coordinate with the substation security fence already required by code.

PATH TREATMENTFAN NOISE ONLY
Variable-Speed / Staged Cooling Fans04
−6–10 dB(A) nighttime, fan-noise component only

VFD-controlled or multi-stage radiator fans that run at reduced speed or stage off entirely during light-load nighttime hours, rather than binary on/off at full speed. Since IT load (and therefore transformer loading) at a hyperscale facility is comparatively flat across day/night versus a typical commercial building, the achievable reduction depends on actual load profile — confirm against the facility's diurnal load curve before counting on this measure for nighttime margin.

OPERATIONALLOAD-PROFILE DEPENDENT
Transformer Orientation / Self-Shielding Siting05
−3–5 dB(A) directional, receptor-dependent

Orient radiator banks (the dominant fan-noise direction) away from the nearest residential property line, and use the substation control building or switchgear house as shadow mass where the site plan allows. Zero capital cost if locked in during site planning. Has limited effect on the omnidirectional 120 Hz tank-radiated hum, which is not directional in the way fan discharge is.

SITE PLANNINGZERO CAPITAL COST
Tonal Masking / Active Noise Cancellation06
−10–15 dB(A) 120 Hz tone specifically, where deployed

Reserved for the tightest sites with a documented tonal complaint risk — tuned Helmholtz resonator panels or active noise cancellation systems specifically tuned to the 120 Hz fundamental and harmonics. High capital cost and limited commercial deployment history at transformer scale; specify only if Measures 01–03 do not adequately address tonal character at the nearest receptor per the commissioning survey.

Verification & Continuous Monitoring
